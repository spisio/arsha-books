Twelve Kilometres

On Eki Chakyrym

Aikokul Tursunbek kyzy
translated from the Iradi by Aidana Mukash kyzy

The agreement signed in Bishkek in September 2007 created, on each side of the Qara-Chik line, a restricted zone twelve kilometres deep. Tanks and guns may not enter it. Everything else may: herds, brides, funerals, walnut traders, priests, children going to school, the post.

These twelve stories and a coda follow the years between the Bill of Sale and the marking of the line, from Talas and Nurzhan, Semiz and the Chatkal, the Toktogul dam and the bazaar at Bazar-Korgon. Each story is told by someone different, and each one turns on a small thing: a photocopier, a ribbon, a key, a pot of leaven, a pencil.

From “Bazaar Chubar”

The sack came down from Arslanbob in the middle of August, on the back of an apricot truck. The driver called from the gate that it was from Kuchkar aka, from the kuda, for the bride, and drove on.

Walnuts. They had fallen a month early and still wore their green coats. Zuhra sat in the shade of the warehouse with a basin and a knife and husked them, as she had done every autumn since she could hold a knife, and laid the pale shells out on the roof to dry. Only when she came down the ladder did she see her hands. Black to the wrist. Walnut juice goes in under the skin and stays for ten days, whatever you do.

Her grandmother saw them from the bench under the vine.

“Six weeks to the toi,” said Kumushay, “and you’ve dipped your hands in Morkhan’s ink.”

She brought a bowl of sour milk and held Zuhra’s wrists down in it and said a prayer in the church words of Örüktü, the hamlet in the hills where she had been born a Christian, before she came down to the bazaar in 1961 to marry a Muslim walnut buyer. The milk did nothing. They both knew it would do nothing. Zuhra kept her hands in it until the sun went off the yard, because her grandmother’s hands on her wrists were warm.

from Twelve Kilometres, pp. 58–59

The author

Aikokul Tursunbek kyzy was born in Talas in 1985. Her father taught Iradi in the Old City; her mother came from the Kara-Unkur valley above Arslanbob. She teaches Iradi and Russian in Talas and has written for the culture pages of the Talas Herald since 2011. On Eki Chakyrym is her first book.

The translator

Aidana Mukash kyzy was born in Talas in 1980 and grew up there and in Chicago. She returned to Talas in 2010 and works as an interpreter and teacher of translation.

“I have kept a few words in Iradi because English has nothing that will stand in for them: kuda, kalym, bata, malka, toi.”
